Teaching a Dominion player to buy better by searching through the shuffles it can't see

A light version of AlphaZero for the card game Dominion (base set, 2nd edition, Steam rules). At every buy it runs an information-set Monte Carlo tree search that deals the hidden cards anew in each of its 400 simulations, and a small neural network guides the search and learns from it in self-play. The goal: win 65% of 1,000 fresh games head to head against the evolved GA genome it started from, with the same play of action cards on both sides so only the buys differ. Passed at iteration 20 (65%) and held at 69–70% from iteration 100. Twenty variants since then (more search, softer priors, an honest value head, AlphaZero's value-only leaves) all stayed at 66–70%: about 70% is this method's ceiling here. Training has stopped; the Lessons, Study and Podcast tabs tell the story.

What this is

Dominion is a deck-building card game. Each turn you play the cards in your hand, count your coins and buy one card for your deck: treasure (Copper $0, Silver $3, Gold $6) to buy more later, victory cards (Estate, Duchy, Province $8) that score but clog your hand, or one of 10 action cards dealt for this game (the kingdom) out of the 26 in the base set. The game ends when the Provinces or any three piles run out; most victory points wins.

A concrete example. Turn 9, you have $6. Gold makes every later turn richer; Duchy is 3 points now and a dead card in every later hand; Witch ($5) would hand your opponent a Curse (−1 point) each time you play it. Which is best depends on how far the game is from ending, what the opponent is doing and which cards you will draw next, which you can't see. That is the decision this project learns. Every other decision (which action to play, what to trash, what to gain with Workshop) is made by the evolved genome of dominion-v1, on both sides of every comparison.

The measure. 1,000 games against the GA base genome: 500 kingdoms never used in training or in the per-iteration evaluation, each played twice with the seats swapped. A win counts 1, a shared win ½. The 95% interval (Wilson) on 1,000 games is about ±3 points. The GA itself scores 50% by definition; the target was 65%.

Why it is hard. Dominion hides information (your draw order, the opponent's hand) and is noisy: two similar players stay close to a coin flip until late, and a single game result says little about any one buy. The GA genome is a strong opponent: a priority list evolved over millions of games, specialised per kind of kingdom.

Approach

Start from the GA: 12,000 of its games teach a small network (175 inputs → 128 → 128 → 34 buy logits + a win estimate; 43,555 numbers) to copy its buys. Then loop. Each iteration plays 1,000 self-play games in which every buy is chosen by a search: 400 simulations, each one shuffling the hidden cards anew, walking a tree of buy decisions guided by the net, and scoring the position at its edge by half the net's win estimate and half the result of finishing the game with the GA genome. The net then learns, from the last 5 iterations' games, to predict the buy the search chose and who won. A fixed evaluation (800 games) runs every iteration and a goal check on fresh games every 10.

Real numbers (8-core laptop CPU, i5-8250U): one simulation costs about 88 µs, so a searched game takes 0.8 s; an iteration (1,000 self-play games on 7 workers + 800 evaluation games + training) takes about 4 minutes. Run r2 did 357 iterations in 24 hours.
